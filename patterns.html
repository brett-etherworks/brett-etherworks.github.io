Design Patterns

From Manifesto to Practice

I believe interactive non-fiction can play a role in building what comes next. This dissertation has contributed a series of research-creation case studies which have provided a path for using culture jamming, speculation and play to imagine the Internet otherwise. The final contribution of my doctoral research is not a manifesto, but instead a series of design patterns that will both guide my work going forward. I offer them for future practitioners to remix and make their own.

Rather than rules, these five maxims to myself are more akin to reminders for when I am stuck or losing faith, inspired by generative prompts such as Brian Eno and Peter Schmidt's Oblique Strategies. "Give the game away," "Abandon normal instruments," and "Use fewer notes" are illustrative examples of the 100 cards, which Eno and Schmidt made both for themselves and for other artists to think through creative blocks. The following design patterns are similar guideposts to remind me that my goal is not to craft the perfect argument for why a better Internet is needed and possible, but to make that realization joyful. These design patterns are drawn from the research-creation projects that built this dissertation, those that came in the decades before, and the contributions of the scholars and artists who inspired the literature of my doctoral studies.

  • 3. Meet at the Mall
  • 4. Jam Yourself

1. Design for Play

☞ Build experiences that invite people into unfamiliar relationships with digital systems. The work should feel alive enough that people want to enter it—even when, or especially when, they do not yet know they are entering a critique.

When systems feel opaque, extractive, or inevitable, play becomes a way to make their logics graspable without reducing them to didactic explanations. Designing for play means building experiences that invite people into unfamiliar relationships with digital systems through mimicry, chance, disorientation, and friendly competition.

As I learned through making BrainRot Revolution, play shifts publics from resignation to participation: publics must do something to experience the work. In the game, players needed to meet the goal of overfeeding Shoggoth with its own memes until it exploded—the game's ludonarrative was congruent with the theme of creatively destroying the Internet. My aim was not to trivialize the stakes of digital life, but to open affective and embodied routes into questions that can otherwise feel abstract or overwhelming. And like Welcome to the Metaverse, where players experienced the disorientation of trying on different faces or getting a fake facelift, the play was uncomfortable and funny at once. When designing for play, the work should feel alive enough that people want to enter it, even when (or especially when) they do not yet know they are entering a critique.

The Education of Ursula taught me that speculative imagination is its own register of play. In adapting The Thing From the Future, Ursula gave players random prompts and asked them to describe objects from the futures they were inventing as they spoke. The pleasure was in the combinations themselves. Even when the conversational frame faltered, the game worked—the lesson I carry forward is that imaginative play, paired with the pleasure of learning and mastering the rules of the game, can convene publics around futures they did not arrive ready to articulate.

Beyond my own work, I look for inspiration in the ways that other Internet activists have created games such as Monster Match, a dating app simulator that reveals how homophily-based algorithms inevitably recommend dates to the people who most closely resemble them. Or Survival of The Best Fit, an educational game about bias in hiring AI where you play an overworked HR professional who turns to automated systems. As the makers of SOTBF suggest, games can play a powerful role in "helping people understand the technology, and demand more accountability from those building increasingly pervasive software systems."

2. Make Friction Fun(ny)

☞ Design works that make the absurdities of online life more laughable. Funny friction operates as an invitation rather than a complaint.

In contemporary digital systems, any design that leads to delay or hesitation threatens engagement, and interfaces are built to keep people gliding along an optimised path. Disrupting these systems requires moving in the opposite direction. Deliberate friction slows things down and creates micro-opportunities for awareness. When friction is paired with humour, its critical edge becomes more inviting. Creating awkward interfaces and intentional glitches can remind publics that systems are constructed rather than inevitable, and that their logics can therefore be contested.

Making friction funny keeps those interruptions from hardening into scolding or moralism and invites people to linger in them. Designing Friction authors Moniker offer a case study with Performative Conversations, a video series by founders Luna Maurer and Roel Watters who staged conversations about the Internet while filming their painted yellow faces with a camera attached to a helmet. The resulting facial images are examined by computer vision and compared to emojis, which are composited side by side. The resulting absurd images make strange our use of emojis and the performative nature of online conversations intended for other people to witness. It's the humour that makes it work: "Real emotions seep through the yellow painted faces and their authority on the subject matter seems less founded than they thought."

Another example of friction is found in the Internet Phone Book, a simple work of curation that is amusing in its throwback notion that one could fit all there is to know about the Internet into a single book. Yet the way the book contrasts everything about our current Internet by being modest, pleasurable, and intentionally slow to consume uses funny friction to make us wonder why parts of the Internet couldn't still feel this way. As its designers write, the book "is a step back to the 90s, before the invention of search engines and social media feeds… but it is also a step into the future by birthing a new infrastructure for building relations and publishing online outside social media. It is a reminder that the Internet is ultimately something we make and that we could as easily make it differently."

This "new infrastructure" that the Internet Phone Book's authors imagine reads to me more like a recommitment to the open internet—the public agora created through open communication standards and an animating spirit of democratic participation. Like all aspects of democratic life, it requires work—friction—to make the Internet a place again. Humour disarms, and in doing so, opens publics to alternatives.

3. Meet at the Mall

☞ Where the work appears matters as much as what it says. Meeting people at the mall, on Instagram, in Fortnite, or in Minecraft says: this is for you, and you can shape it.

The publics I most want to move from resignation to engagement are already gathered—at bus stops, in grocery lines, and yes, at the mall. They don't need me to host them in galleries and lecture halls; my job is to find them where they already are. This was why staging BrainRot Revolution at the failed shoe store was impactful: people were already there, and once invited to participate, were able to see the parallels with such pseudo-public spaces and the ways that the Internet has been transformed into a site of almost constant consumption.

My project, then, is to meet these already assembled publics where they are and to smuggle small, playful interruptions into their everyday environments to spark new conversations about the Internet and what else it could become. This pattern commits me to treating venue selection as a form of world-building—and not just in physical spaces. In the same way that Welcome to the Metaverse re-imagined people's Instagram feeds as a forum for a documentary, I need to continue asking where on the Internet the works should appear, and potentially even crafting new online spaces modelled after the Internet as I wish it to be.

I am inspired in this effort by the ways activists from other causes have used digital spaces in non-traditional ways. The International Committee for the Red Cross remixed the hugely popular free-to-play game Fortnite to have players compete to see how many people they could heal in a battle royale rather than how many they could kill—teaching players about the rules of war while allowing gamers to donate to the ICRC via in-game purchases. As a result, the ICRC reported a 37% growth in online donations: their best-performing campaign ever. Reporters Without Borders created The Uncensored Library, a virtual library on a Minecraft server that players could enter even in countries practicing state-based censorship, where censored journalism was rendered illegible by being turned into game art—and therefore escaped notice.

The reminder I carry forward is that where the work appears matters as much as what it says. Fancy festivals and exclusive talks signal one thing; meeting people at the mall, on Instagram, in Fortnite, or in Minecraft says something else: this is for you, and you can shape it.

4. Jam Yourself

☞ Don't leave the lie on the table. Make the liner notes a part of the jam—design works in which the apparatus shows through.

As I finished this dissertation, I had an occasion to speak with Mark Hosler, one of the founders of Negativland. He was feeling skeptical about whether the techniques he helped pioneer had any relevance today.

"How does culture jamming work when no one trusts anything? These days, we are not disagreeing about the interpretation of what facts mean—facts themselves have become fungible, and they are presented in total absence of context. In an AI world, everything collapses into a generated fact that may or may not actually be true, but it is presented as if it is." — M. Hosler, personal communication, July 11, 2025

Despite his sadness at seeing his own techniques wielded by reactionary forces—"Trump is like a Darth Vader culture jammer!", he lamented—Mark took pains to point out that Negativland's techniques always included a moment of letting the audience see how they had been manipulated: "The thing is, when we would do a media prank or a culture jam, there had to be a reveal… we weren't saying the news was fake, we were saying 'use discernment'."

In a similarly reflective mood, Stephen Duncombe asked culture jammers the Yes Men how media pranks—such as their staging of a New York Times special edition claiming the Iraq War was over while in truth it still raged—differed from the "alternative facts" of the Trump administration. The Yes Men replied: "we don't let the lie stay on the table."

As creators confronting our deep complicities with the technologies and information infrastructures we can't escape, we may be forced to reveal the limits of the methods we use, surface the biases of the models we rely on, disclose the conditions of production, and create scenarios in which our own authority is destabilized. The Education of Ursula taught me what jamming myself looks like in practice—and the dangers of not doing so. The project surfaced three bad habituses I had not been able to see while inside the work: the disclosure habitus (trusting that naming a problem within a work substitutes for structuring the work to expose it), the ventriloquist habitus (using another person's body as a memetic container for affect I had not earned), and the god trick habitus (uncritically adopting an LLM's positionless output as a substitute for situated, embodied analysis). Each was a way of failing to jam myself.

Jamming myself means designing works in which the apparatus shows through: where the prompts guiding an AI, the datasets being scraped, or the moderation decisions of a platform become part of the story rather than invisible scaffolding. One way out of Hosler's bind lies in the ethos of the open web. Early web culture assumed the ability to inspect how web pages were made—a viewer could use the browser's "view source" function to see how the page was constructed. A vernacular of linking outwards to other pages built a culture of citation in which the quality of the linked source mattered. Wikipedia operationalises that sensibility at the level of public knowledge—its most important interface is arguably not the article page, but the surrounding apparatus: the citations, the "talk" page where disagreements are argued in public, and the revision history where every change is legible, attributable, and reversible.

If culture jamming once relied on liner notes, then open-web design suggests a contemporary equivalent: works that build their liner notes into the interaction itself, where audiences can inspect the provenance of claims, see what was edited or generated, and trace the conditions under which a statement became "fact." Don't leave the lie on the table, and make the liner notes a part of the jam.

5. Practise Hope

☞ Keep treading water. Practising hope is a refusal of despair—a commitment to keep building small, speculative, playful spaces where publics can feel that the internet is still a place worth fighting over.

Hope is the primary element embedded in any practice of futures-making: why invite people into possible worlds unless you believe better ones remain possible, even if you cannot yet describe them? Of all the thinkers I came across while creating Necessary Tomorrows, one feels particularly important to this final design pattern. The prison abolitionist Mariame Kaba, reflecting on how hope is not a synonym for optimism, clarified that hope is not an emotion at all: "Hope doesn't preclude feeling sadness or frustration or anger or any other emotion that makes total sense. Hope isn't an emotion, you know? Hope is not optimism… Hope is a discipline; we have to practice it every single day."

Practising hope allows me to continue to act when the infrastructures around me feel fragile, extractive, or collapsing. It draws on the speculative sensibilities of experiential futures, the ethical reflexivity of feminist technoscience, and the understanding that publics form not only through critique but also through shared emotion and experience. Hope is an orientation that refuses both technological fatalism and naive futurism.

My fellow Internet enthusiast Cory Doctorow reminds me that hope is action—it is a way of keeping the internet alive: "An optimist decides not to equip the Titanic with lifeboats because it is unsinkable. A pessimist doesn't bother to swim when the ship sinks and is lost at sea. To be hopeful is to tread water because so long as you haven't gone to the bottom, rescue is still possible. It's not a sure thing, but everyone who gave up sank, and everyone who was fished out the sea kept treading water. Hope is the necessary, but insufficient, precondition for survival."

The four rules of the manifesto I wrote in 2008 believed that passion would be enough to gather publics. These five patterns are what I have learned in the years since: that publics gather not around clarity of argument, but around shared imagining. Hope is the discipline that makes that imagination possible.

The works I have made in this dissertation, and the works this practice is in conversation with, are all expressions of hope for the Internet. This final pattern for my work going forward, and for all others who care about the Internet, is to keep treading water. When faced with the dead internet theory, model collapse, and the broader sense that so much is falling apart, it could be tempting to let despair set the horizon of what is thinkable. Practising hope is a refusal of that temptation. It is a commitment to keep building small, speculative, playful spaces where publics can feel, however briefly, that the internet is still a place worth fighting over, and that they are not alone in that fight.
