Design Patterns

From Manifesto to Practice

Rather than a manifesto, my practice going forward is grounded in a set of design patterns. In lieu of rules, these five maxims to myself are more akin to reminders for when I am stuck or losing faith, inspired by generative prompts such as Brian Eno's Oblique Strategies. "Give the game away," "Abandon normal instruments," and "Use fewer notes" are illustrative examples of the 100 cards which Eno made for himself and other artists to help him through moments where he was stuck creatively.

The following design patterns are similar guideposts to remind me that my goal is not to craft the perfect argument for why a better Internet is needed and possible, but to make that realization joyful.

1. Design for Play

When systems feel opaque, extractive, or inevitable, play becomes a way of making their logics graspable without reducing them to didactic explanation. Designing for play means building experiences that invite people into unfamiliar relationships with digital systems through mimicry, chance, disorientation, and friendly competition. Play shifts publics from resignation to participation—publics must do something to experience the work. The aim is not to trivialize the stakes of digital life, but to open affective and embodied routes into questions that can otherwise feel abstract or overwhelming. When designing for play, the work should feel alive enough that people want to enter it, even when (or especially when) they do not yet know they are entering a critique. Internet activists have created games that reveal how algorithmic systems work, helping people understand the technology and demand more accountability from those building increasingly pervasive software systems.

2. Make Friction Fun(ny)

In contemporary digital systems, any design that leads to delay or hesitation threatens engagement, and interfaces are built to keep people gliding along an optimized path. Disrupting these systems requires moving in the opposite direction. Deliberate friction slows things down and creates micro-opportunities for awareness. When friction is paired with humor, its critical edge becomes more inviting. Creating awkward interfaces and intentional glitches can remind publics that systems are constructed rather than inevitable, and that their logics can therefore be contested. Making friction funny keeps those interruptions from hardening into scolding or moralism and invites people to linger in them. When interacting with screens we lack resistance—swiping screens makes our world more superficial. Designing friction requires thinking about how to increase our resistance, how we can engage our hands and whole bodies, and how these bodily engagements bring us together. Humor is disarming. The next stage of Internet activism must start with an assumption that publics and counterpublics no longer need to be formed from reminders that the Internet is broken, but that they can instead be created through shared laughter at its absurdities.

3. Build Publics in Unexpected Venues

The publics that I am most interested in moving from resignation to engagement are unlikely to gather in galleries and lecture halls. If you want to meet people where they are at, semiotic insurgents need to learn to love the mall. By creating something fun in a shopping mall, I was able to turn shoppers into performers and bystanders into collaborators. While the incubation of work at festivals was instrumental in developing concepts and granting conceptual space to consider interventions, it was not fully executed work until it was presented in public. By being situated in unexpected venues like shopping malls, work dislocates digital critique from the expected venues of art and academia and normalizes speculative practice, attracting unintentional audiences who self-select into counterpublics. The goal is to stage encounters where critical digital literacy can emerge organically rather than through didactic instruction.

4. Jam Yourself

Creating critical work with AI tools requires confronting your own complicity. This pattern asks practitioners to treat their own enthusiasms—including the excitement of new technical affordances—as objects of inquiry rather than as neutral tools. In practical terms, jamming yourself might look like building meta-layers into projects where the system comments on its own operations, inviting collaborators and publics to critique not only the work's subject but its form, and documenting failures and discomforts as part of the research output rather than editing them out. Early web culture assumed the ability to inspect how web pages were made through the browser's "view source" function. A vernacular of linking outwards to other pages built a culture of citation in which the quality of the linked source mattered. If culture jamming once relied on liner notes, then open-web design suggests a contemporary equivalent: works that build their liner notes into the interaction itself, where audiences can inspect the provenance of claims, see what was edited or generated, and trace the conditions under which a statement became "fact." This provides a model for a strong aesthetics of accountability in critical making.

5. Practice Hope

Hope is the primary element embedded in any practice of futures-making: why invite people into possible worlds unless you believe better ones remain possible, even if you cannot yet describe them? When faced with the dead internet theory, model collapse, and the broader sense that so much is falling apart, it could be tempting to let despair set the horizon of what is thinkable. Practicing hope is a refusal of that temptation. It is a commitment to keep building small, speculative, playful spaces where publics can feel, however briefly, that the internet is still a place worth fighting over, and that they are not alone in that fight. The works created in this dissertation, and the works this practice is in conversation with, are all expressions of hope for the Internet. By providing small apertures of escape from how the Internet is now, these creative works lead to larger portals that we can step through—hopefully with fellow travelers. As Mariame Kaba reminds us, hope is a discipline—not optimism, but the active practice of working toward preferable futures even when the odds seem long.
