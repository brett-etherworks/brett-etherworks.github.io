Welcome to the Metaverse

Format: Instagram AR Filter Year: 2023 Platform: Meta SparkAR
"Welcome! Blink if you agree to the terms and conditions!" An enthusiastic voice greets you as your face is scanned. You blink. Your eyes are replaced with a celebration emoji 🎉. Music begins: "We're so excited you've agreed to join The Metaverse!"

An Instagram face filter transforms your own face into an interactive controller for a satirical onboarding ritual into Meta's vision of the Metaverse. Over a few minutes, users blink, smile, and scan their bodies, watching as their faces are augmented with emojis, overlays, and even photos of strangers. Each gesture reveals another layer of extraction, turning personal data into spectacle.

The experience condenses the politics of surveillance capitalism into a playful but unsettling encounter staged directly on your own body. By using Meta's own tools to critique Meta's own ambitions, the work practices what I call context jamming—culture jamming adapted for algorithmically curated feeds where no two users see the same thing.

Documentation: Welcome to the Metaverse filter experience

Context Jamming

Choosing Instagram's AR filters as the distribution platform was a deliberate methodological decision. Much like the culture jammers of the 1980s and 90s who hacked billboards and broadcast media, I sought to intervene in Meta's own ecosystem—using the company's tools to critique its ambitions for the Metaverse.

But unlike a spray-painted billboard or pirated TV broadcast, SparkAR is a tightly controlled, vertically integrated system. Every filter must be uploaded to Meta's servers, pass content moderation, and be explicitly approved before reaching audiences. Each user encounters the filter within their own algorithmically curated feed—no two users see the same context.

From Billboard to Filter Bubble

Whereas culture jammers once hijacked one-to-many distribution to reach collective audiences with a counter-message, interventions on Instagram take place inside personalized feeds. The "channel" is no longer shared. Instead, critique must pass through layers of automated scanning, content moderation, and opaque ranking systems that filter what appears at all.

I call this practice context jamming: creating subversively personalized media for algorithmic systems. Rather than intervening in mass-media culture, the work operates only within a viewer's context. Each experience is slightly different—using a specific player's face, responding to their choices, and appearing alongside different content for different groups of people.

Playing With Your Face

The experience transforms a user's face into an interactive controller. By detecting whether eyes are open or closed, emojis can appear. A smile can turn a dial, while a furrowed brow can make rain appear from a cloud. This literal form of mimicry invites users to try on different faces and characters as the narration progresses.

The work also creates ilinix—disorientation and perceptual instability. When users suddenly see another person's face mapped onto their own, or when their eyes are replaced with emojis, it disturbs the coherence of self-recognition. The vertigo emerges from the sudden estrangement of one's own body as mediated through Meta's tools.

Estrangement and Ambivalence

The result is both amusing and unsettling, producing a mixed affect of laughter and unease. This ambivalence allows users to simultaneously enjoy the creative affordances while becoming critical of the platform's extractive logic. Users must "stay with the trouble" to continue—confronting their complicity while experiencing the spectacle.

Asymmetrical Cognitive Ammunition

Creating Welcome to the Metaverse exposed the limits of critiquing within vertically integrated systems. Upon submission, we received a rejection notice from SparkAR stating we had violated Policy 3.7 by using a trademarked asset or color gradient. There was no indication of which part violated the policy.

The team suspected the violation came from using the blue thumbs-up "like" button. We replaced it with a heart—an image Meta doesn't (yet) own. Twenty-four hours later, the filter was approved.

The irony wasn't lost on us: the entire piece directly criticizes Meta, but only the like button triggered rejection. Our hunch, which we'll never confirm, is that review was undertaken either by an algorithm or an outsourced worker. Neither appears suited to detect satire—both exemplify the extractive stack the Metaverse is built upon.

Ephemeral Interventions

On August 27th, 2024, Meta announced it would no longer support SparkAR. As of January 14, 2025, all user-created content became unavailable. The filter that once lived on Instagram now exists only in documentation. Like a vegan détourning of a McDonald's advertisement, context jamming interventions are fated to be ephemeral.

This ephemerality underscores a central lesson: these works must be timely, striking while the infrastructures they critique still exist, and before platforms rewrite the rules of play.

Design Pattern: Strike While the Infrastructure Exists

Context jamming interventions are necessarily ephemeral. They must be timely—created and deployed while the platforms they critique are still available, before content policies shift or tools are deprecated. Document thoroughly, because the work itself may not survive.

Cultivating Critical Literacy

By staging encounters directly on users' own bodies and devices, Welcome to the Metaverse positions them not as spectators but as participants. Their gestures, expressions, and embodied presence shape the unfolding of the work. Users become both player and subject of critique.

  • Face becomes interface—blinking, smiling, and expressions control the narrative
  • Estrangement through self-recognition disruption creates ambivalence
  • Humor and unease combine to expose surveillance capitalism's intimate reach
  • Context jamming inserts critical messages into personalized algorithmic feeds
  • Ephemerality reflects the precarity of critique within platform capitalism

The work demonstrates that cultivating critical digital literacy requires more than exposing extraction—it requires creating encounters that are joyful, embodied, and reflexive. By turning surveillance into play, the filter makes visible the systems that shape our digital lives, while acknowledging our complicity within them.
