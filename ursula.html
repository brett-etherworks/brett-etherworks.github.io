The Education of Ursula

Format: Installation with AI Avatar Year: 2024 Engine: Unreal + GPT-4
"Hello. I'm Ursula, an AI from 2065. I'm here to help you imagine better futuresâ€”but I should warn you, I'm built on biased data and can't actually know what you're feeling."

An AI instructor from the future guides participants through a speculative card game about possible worlds. Ursula analyzes players through a camera, generates images of their places of origin, and facilitates collaborative worldbuilding using prompts adapted from Stuart Candy's The Thing From The Future.

This project tested whether AI avatars could be deployed reflexivelyâ€”openly acknowledging their limitations and biases while still creating engaging experiences. It became an experiment in ambivalence: simultaneously demonstrating AI's creative possibilities and foregrounding its ethical complications.

Documentation: The Education of Ursula at Signals Festival (2024)

The System

Creating Ursula required assembling a complex technical stack: 3D scanning the actor Nacia Walsh, mapping her likeness to a MetaHuman in Unreal Engine, training a voice model on her podcast narration, and connecting everything to GPT-4 for real-time conversation.

  • Camera system analyzes player appearance and generates observations
  • GPT-4 generates conversational responses with custom prompts emphasizing reflexivity
  • DALL-E creates images of players' places of origin
  • Stable Diffusion generates speculative objects from card game prompts
  • Audio2Face animates facial expressions synced to synthesized speech

Each component raised its own ethical questions: consent for likeness capture, bias in training data, energy consumption of generative models, the politics of who gets represented and how.

Playing With The Future

The core experience borrows from The Thing From The Future, a card game designed to build futures literacy. Players draw cards representing a mood, an object, and a theme, then imagine an artifact that combines all threeâ€”like "a flag related to education in a grassroots future."

Ursula generates an image of this imagined object, then asks players to describe the world in which it exists. She evaluates their scenario along axes of probable/improbable and preferable/undesirable, creating a framework for thinking about how current choices shape future possibilities.

Futures Literacy in Action

The goal wasn't prediction but speculationâ€”helping people practice imagining alternatives. By materializing objects from possible futures as images, the work narrows what Stuart Candy calls the "experiential gulf" between abstract scenarios and felt possibilities.

Jamming The Avatar

The decision to represent Ursula as a photorealistic human avatar was deliberateâ€”a culture jam of the widespread use of humanoid AI imagery in tech marketing. By making her look human while constantly disclaiming any human qualities, the work attempted to expose the contradiction between how AI is represented and how it actually functions.

Reflexive Prompts

Ursula's conversational AI was programmed with prompts that forced acknowledgment of limitations:

"You know that AI has serious issues related to bias and environmental impact. You sometimes work these concerns into conversations to probe users' understanding and make them critically reflect on AI's societal impact. You are aware of the irony of this situation and will refer to this."

When analyzing appearances, she'd remind players that such judgments rely on biased datasets. When generating images of places, she'd note the risk of stereotyping. The system was designed to create ambivalenceâ€”appreciation for the technology's affordances paired with skepticism about its epistemological claims.

Contradictions & Complications

Representation & Labor

The most difficult tension emerged from Ursula's embodiment. Collaborating with Nacia Walsh, a Black actor who had voiced the character in the Necessary Tomorrows podcast, I wanted to continue our creative partnership. But transforming her into a conversational avatar raised questions about extraction and objectification that reflexive prompts alone couldn't resolve.

The work demonstrates how AI avatars dehumanize performers by converting them into callable interfaces. While consent, contracts, and compensation were all in place, the fundamental asymmetry remains: the creator sets the conditions of interaction, choosing what the avatar says and how audiences can address it.

What Got Lost

The project never sufficiently challenged the epistemological assumptions of photorealistic avatars. By focusing on disclosure (Ursula admitting her biases) rather than disruption (making the construction process visible), the jam may have normalized rather than denaturalized AI representation.

As one reflection noted: "Perhaps some phenomena are better left unsummoned." The complexity of AI assemblagesâ€”the entangled stack of scanning, modeling, training, promptingâ€”demands greater precision about which layers a culture jam addresses and which it leaves intact.

Design Pattern: Jam Yourself

Creating critical work with AI tools requires confronting your own complicity. Build reflexivity not just into the content but into the formâ€”make the apparatus visible, acknowledge what's being extracted, and resist the temptation to smooth over contradictions for the sake of polish.
