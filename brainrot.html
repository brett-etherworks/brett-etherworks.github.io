BrainRot Revolution

Format: Motion-Controlled Arcade Game Year: 2025 Engine: Unity + Motion Tracking
"Sometimes it does feel like when you are just going on the internet and you are doom scrolling...it does kind of feel like you're just falling into the abyss sometimes." — Player 3

A motion-controlled arcade game where players use their bodies to help a tentacled AI monster named Shoggoth consume internet memes. Move side to side to aim, raise your arms to fire colored bubbles at clusters of viral content. Match three memes and they disappear. Clear the screen and—BOOM—the internet explodes.

Then comes the question: "You blew up the Internet! What should we build instead?"

Installed in a shopping mall in Victoria, BC, BrainRot Revolution was played thousands of times by teenagers, seniors, tourists, and families. It became an experiment in using play to cultivate ambivalence—simultaneously celebrating and critiquing the absurdity of algorithmic culture while asking participants to imagine alternatives.

Documentation: BrainRot Revolution at the Bay Centre

Model Collapse as Metaphor

The game emerged from exploring model collapse—a phenomenon in AI where systems trained on synthetic data begin to degrade in quality. Just as AI models risk deteriorating when they recycle their own outputs, our collective imaginaries suffer when visions of the future are endlessly remixed from familiar scenarios.

Model collapse became both technical failure and cultural metaphor: the moment when our tools for imagining the future fail us. "Brain rot" extends this further—referring to both the perceived cognitive impact of consuming low-quality algorithmic content and the absurdist online vernacular that emerges from it.

Brain Rot Language

Phrases like "Skibidi Toilet" and slang like "Ohio" illustrate a cultural inversion where online discourse no longer mirrors events IRL but increasingly defines them. This evolving language reveals the extent to which digital absurdity now shapes mainstream imagination.

Memes as Internet Content

The bubbles players destroy contain five iconic memes representing a cross-section of digital culture in 2025:

  • This is Fine — Question Hound appearing content while his room burns, expressing ironic nonchalance
  • Wojak — Bald figure staring into distance, embodying existential dread
  • Pepe the Frog — Matt Furie's character appropriated by various online communities
  • Doge — Shiba Inu expressing enthusiasm in broken English
  • Loss — Four-panel comic reduced to abstract minimalism

These memes represent the content that AI models (represented by Shoggoth) ingest to train their algorithms—algorithms which in turn produce more content. The game makes literal the feedback loop of cultural production and consumption in algorithmic media.

Game Jam Praxis

BrainRot Revolution was created during the Immersive Jam—a two-week workshop organized by Hololabs and the City of Victoria. Given the tight timeline, we based the project on an existing genre: bubble shooter games, where players launch colored projectiles to create matches and clear the playfield.

Shoggoth as Player Character

We chose Shoggoth—H.P. Lovecraft's amorphous, tentacled creature—as the character players control. In contemporary AI discourse, "Shoggoth" has become internet slang for large language models: vast, alien intelligences that companies attempt to mask with friendly chatbot interfaces.

Players control Shoggoth's tentacles by moving their bodies side to side (ideally with hands on hips), while raising their arms fires bubbles. The physicality creates an embodied connection to the abstract process of AI consumption and generation.

A Spoonful of Sugar

BrainRot Revolution was almost universally reported as fun to play. The physicality, humor, and spectacle drew thousands of participants who might not otherwise engage with critical work about technology.

"The fact that something on the screen is reacting to you just moving as opposed to a joystick—there's less of an intermediary between you and the game. That's kind of neat." — Player 1
"I like the movement aspect. So it's a whole body experience. It's not just sitting and moving a mouse." — Player 5

The game avoided "preaching to the converted" by not preaching at all. Players reported feeling good about the experience while simultaneously reflecting on unhealthy aspects of their internet use. The pleasure of play created space for critical reflection rather than defensive rejection.

Confronting Bad Habitus

When discussing "brain rot" and how the game made them reflect on content consumption, players revealed negative associations with their current online behavior:

"Sometimes when you are doom scrolling...it does kind of feel like you're just falling into the abyss." — Player 3
"[Brain rot is a] lack of critical thinking and sort of a willingness to let yourself fall prey to whatever the internet wants to throw at you." — Player 4

The game created permission to acknowledge these feelings without shame. By externalizing brain rot as something to destroy (literally blowing up the internet), players could confront their complicity playfully rather than defensively.

Social Witnessing

Many players experienced BrainRot as a social intervention. Though designed for competitive play, most participants collaborated rather than competed—helping each other aim shots and celebrating victories together. The public setting in a mall created shared witnessing, with passersby becoming curious and joining in with minimal friction.

Speculative Collapse

The final moment transforms the game from playful destruction to speculative provocation. When players clear the screen, Shoggoth collapses, the internet explodes in a spectacular animation, and text appears:

"You blew up the Internet! What should we build instead?"

This closing prompt opens a horizon of uncertainty without instruction. The work doesn't define a preferred future—it creates space for participants to articulate one, recognizing they're both shaped by and implicated in digital infrastructures.

Narrowing the Experiential Gulf

The collapse sequence provides a concrete, felt event that makes abstract speculation tangible. By grounding what it might feel like for the internet to explode within embodied experience, the work narrows Stuart Candy's "experiential gulf"—transforming speculation from intellectual exercise into visceral possibility.

Making Publics Through Play

BrainRot Revolution cultivated counterpublics in the fashion Michael Warner imagined: a discursive space operating in tension with dominant media, where participants rehearse alternative ways of relating and imagining together.

  • Thousands of plays over four months in a public shopping mall
  • Players ranged from children to seniors, tourists to locals
  • Collaboration emerged organically despite competitive design
  • Fun created permission for critical reflection on digital habits
  • Speculative ending prompted conversations about internet alternatives

Rather than inviting audiences to "add themselves" to a pre-set narrative, the game created conditions for spontaneous encounters between strangers asked to consider meaningful questions about the internet's role in public life. The work acts as public pedagogy—fostering civic engagement through play rather than exposition.

Design Pattern: Make it Fun First

Critical work doesn't require abandoning pleasure. By prioritizing joyful, embodied interaction, BrainRot reached audiences who would reject didactic messaging. The physicality and humor created permission to reflect without defensiveness. Make the medicine taste good.
