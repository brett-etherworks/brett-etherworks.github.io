About This Research

PhD Dissertation • Simon Fraser University, 2025

Abstract

This dissertation explores how interactive documentary can be used to cultivate critically literate digital publics. Drawing from over two decades of practice-based media production, I use research-creation to investigate how approaches rooted in speculation, culture jamming and play can help audiences understand and reimagine the social, ethical, and political implications of networked technologies such as the Internet and Artificial Intelligence.

At the centre of my dissertation is a case study of three interactive documentaries: Welcome To The Metaverse, The Education of Ursula, and BrainRot Revolution. These varied experiences invite reflection on how power circulates through data, platforms, and design, while embracing the joy of imagining preferable futures.

My approach is informed by traditions of documentary, futures studies, and critical digital literacy. I take inspiration from artists and scholars who are making reflexive work using digital systems to explore and critique these same systems. From remixing popular game genres to context jamming platforms like Instagram, my work inserts critical messages directly into the media ecosystems they critique.

Background & Motivation

This work emerges from a career spent making documentaries about digital culture. In 2008, I released Rip! A Remix Manifesto, a feature film celebrating the promise of the internet as a democratizing force for culture. Nearly two decades later, that optimism feels naive. The internet I championed has become a site of surveillance capitalism, algorithmic manipulation, and platform monopolies.

Rather than despair, this dissertation asks: how can we use the same creative tools—remix, speculation, play—to build critically literate publics who can imagine and work toward better digital futures? The answer, I argue, lies in interactive documentary as a form of research-creation that makes abstract systems tangible, contestable, and reimaginable.

Research Questions

The dissertation addresses three interconnected questions:

RQ1: How can creative researchers leverage the elements of interactive documentary to cultivate critically literate digital counterpublics?

RQ2: How can interactive documentaries use speculative methods to create experiential futures that reimagine dominant assumptions about the digital world?

RQ3: How can we culture jam today's media ecologies?

Theoretical Framework

This work draws on four main theoretical traditions:

Interactive Documentary: Building on work by Aston & Gaudenzi, Ellis, and Rose, I explore how interactive forms can convene publics around critical engagement with digital systems.

Speculative Design: Following Dunne & Raby, Bleecker, and Wakkary & Oogjes, I use speculation to make the familiar strange and open space for imagining alternative digital futures.

Culture Jamming: Drawing on Debord, Eco, and Duncombe, I adapt culture jamming tactics for an era where the medium itself—platforms, algorithms, AI—is the message.

Futures Studies: Informed by Candy, Dator, and Miller, I use experiential futures to build futures literacy and help publics imagine preferable rather than merely probable futures.

Methodology: Research-Creation

This dissertation employs research-creation as its core methodology. As Chapman & Sawchuk describe, research-creation treats creative practice not as illustration of theory but as a primary mode of knowledge production. The three interactive documentaries at the center of this work—Welcome to the Metaverse, The Education of Ursula, and BrainRot Revolution—are both objects of study and tools for inquiry.

Each project was developed iteratively, presented to public audiences at festivals and other venues, and analyzed reflexively for what it revealed about convening counterpublics, building critical digital literacy, and jamming contemporary media ecologies.

Key Contributions

  • Context Jamming: A method for intervening in algorithmic media by jamming not just messages but the contexts in which data flows
  • Design Patterns for Critical Making: Five principles (Design for Play, Make Friction Funny, Build Publics in Unexpected Venues, Jam Yourself, Practice Hope) for creating work that cultivates critical digital literacy
  • Ambivalent AI Design: A framework for creating AI-powered work that foregrounds uncertainty, bias, and the constructed nature of "intelligence"
  • Experiential Futures for Digital Literacy: Methods for using speculation and play to make abstract digital systems tangible and contestable

From Manifesto to Practice

In 2008, Rip! A Remix Manifesto ended with four rules:

1. Culture always builds on the past.
2. The past always tries to control the future.
3. Our future is becoming less free.
4. To build free societies you must limit the control of the past.

This dissertation represents an evolution from that certainty. Instead of a manifesto, I offer design patterns—flexible, situated, hopeful approaches to making work that helps people imagine and build better digital futures. It's rooted in a simple conviction: that the way we build literacy about digital systems should itself be joyful, embodied, and imaginative.
